Getting it right

How Far Can a Blade Sign Stick Out?

Two things decide it, and both come from the city rather than from taste. The sign can only project so far over the public sidewalk, and it has to clear a set height above it, usually around eight feet, so nobody walks into it. On a narrow sidewalk that limits how big the blade can be; on a wide one you have more room. Where your storefront sits, and how much sidewalk is in front of it, drives the whole design.

Service, not a rebuild

What Actually Goes Wrong With a Blade Sign

Blade signs rarely fail all at once. They fail in a pattern, and the pattern tells you whether it's a quick fix or a remake.

The mount, not the sign

  • The bracket loosens or rusts where it meets the wall, and the sign starts to tilt or swing.
  • On brick or stucco, old anchors pull as the wall weathers. That's a re-anchor, not a new sign.
  • A sign hung too high or too small to read from the sidewalk, which is a sizing problem, not a fault.

The face and the light

  • Water gets into an illuminated face and a section goes dark or clouds over.
  • Sun fades a printed or painted face over the years until the contrast drops.
  • On a lit blade, a driver or module fails and one side stops lighting evenly.

Recognize one of these on your sign? That's usually a service call, not a replacement.

Adding one on

Adding a Blade Sign to an Existing Storefront

Most blade signs go onto a building that's already there, so the mount matters as much as the design. Brick, stucco, and a metal canopy each take a different anchor, and a heavier blade on an older wall sometimes needs a quick structural check before it goes up. If you lease, the projection usually needs the landlord's sign-off too, since it hangs over the shared frontage. We sort all of that out before anything is built, so install day is just install day.

The legal part

Permits for Projecting Signs in LA

A blade sign hangs over the public right-of-way, so it carries requirements a flat wall sign never deals with. Los Angeles requires a sign permit through LADBS, and because the sign projects over the sidewalk, often an encroachment approval on top of it. If the blade is illuminated, it's an electrical installation too, which adds an electrical permit. We handle the drawings and the submissions as part of the project, so the sign goes up legally and stays up. See more on sign permitting in Los Angeles.

Good to know

Frequently Asked Questions About Blade Signs in Los Angeles

What's the difference between a blade sign and a projecting sign?

None, the terms are used interchangeably. Both describe a sign that mounts perpendicular to the wall and reaches out over the sidewalk. You'll also hear them called fin signs or hanging signs.

Do I need a permit for a blade sign in Los Angeles?

Yes. Because it projects over the public sidewalk, a blade sign needs a sign permit through LADBS and often an encroachment approval as well. An illuminated blade adds an electrical permit. We prepare and submit all of it as part of the job.

How far can a blade sign stick out, and how high does it have to be?

Both are set by Los Angeles code and depend on your sidewalk. The sign has to clear a minimum height above the walkway, usually around eight feet, and can only project so far over it. We size the blade to what your specific frontage allows.

Can a blade sign be illuminated?

Yes. Blade signs can be halo-lit or backlit so they read after dark. We build the lit ones as UL-listed electric signs with the wiring placed where it can be reached for service later.

Can you mount one on brick, stucco, or a canopy?

All three, each with the right anchor for the surface. A heavier blade on an older wall sometimes needs a quick structural check first, which we handle before install day.

Should it be double-sided?

On a two-way sidewalk, usually yes. A double-sided blade reads to people approaching from both directions, which is the whole reason to put a projecting sign up in the first place.
